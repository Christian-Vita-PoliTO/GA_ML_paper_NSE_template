\begin{abstract}
The identification of a multi-group energy structure for full-core deterministic
simulations is a combinatorial problem whose objective can only be evaluated by
running the reference solver, so that a thorough exploration of the design space
is prohibitively expensive. We replace that evaluation with a neural metamodel
and keep the reference code as the final judge. The metamodel is a hierarchical,
five-network ensemble trained on 91\,723 full-core evaluations of the NEA
lead-cooled fast reactor benchmark performed with the neutronics module of
FRENETIC; it predicts fifteen fitness functions at once---five physical
quantities for each of three control-rod configurations---with a mean
coefficient of determination of $0.99634$ on an external validation set and a
median relative error of $0.403\%$, and it evaluates a candidate structure
several orders of magnitude faster than the solver. One hundred independent
genetic searches then take $79$~s in total, and the fifteen best distinct
candidates are verified with FRENETIC. The resulting 18-group structure is
better than every one of the 124\,693 configurations computed to date, by
$15.8\%$, and better than a structure with uniformly spaced boundaries and the
same number of groups by four orders of magnitude. Two findings qualify the
approach rather than advertise it. First, the residual error of the metamodel is
concentrated in a specific regime---small values of the reactivity and kinetics
fitness functions---and we show by exclusion that it is not label noise, not
data sparsity and not a deficiency of the input
representation, but a property of the target: displacing a single group boundary
changes it by half a decade there, against two hundredths of a decade elsewhere.
Second, the search is systematically drawn towards the regions where the
metamodel is optimistic, which is precisely why the verification stage cannot be
dispensed with. The design space is found to contain two genuinely separated
families of solutions, one of them $16\%$ worse than the other, and roughly half
of the searches converge to it.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Full-core deterministic simulations of fast reactors are performed on a
condensed energy mesh. The number of groups and, more importantly, the placement
of their boundaries determine how faithfully the condensed calculation
reproduces a fine-group or Monte Carlo reference, and the dependence is neither
smooth nor intuitive: two structures with the same number of groups can differ
by orders of magnitude in accuracy. The impact of energy collapsing on the
integral parameters of a fast system has been analysed in detail for the neutron
lifetime~\cite{collapsing}. The problem of choosing the structure itself has
been attacked repeatedly with metaheuristics---particle swarm optimization for
thermal lattices~\cite{psoThermal}, genetic algorithms for multigroup searches
in SIMMER~\cite{massoneGA,simmerGA} and for the ARC fusion
reactor~\cite{arcGA}, simulated annealing for sodium-cooled fast
reactors~\cite{sfrSA}, hierarchical division~\cite{hierDiv}, and dedicated
studies for specific systems~\cite{scwr}.

All of these share one difficulty. The fitness of a candidate structure can only
be established by running the reference calculation, and every generation of a
population-based search demands hundreds of such runs. In the present case one
evaluation of a single structure with the neutronics module of
FRENETIC~\cite{frenetic,freneticBench}---three control-rod configurations of the
NEA lead-cooled fast reactor benchmark~\cite{neaBench}---costs $46$~s on three
cores, so that a
single genetic search of one hundred individuals over one hundred generations
costs about $383$ core-hours. That is affordable once. It is not affordable a
hundred times, which is what a trustworthy exploration of a multimodal design
space requires.

The natural response is to replace the expensive evaluation with a cheap
approximation. Purely physical approximations have been proposed, based on
contributon transport theory rather than on data~\cite{pbfa,contributon}.
Data-driven surrogates have entered the field more recently: neural and
random-forest classifiers have been used to decide whether a given twenty-group
structure is adequate for light-water lattice calculations~\cite{mlClassify},
and a reinforcement-learning agent guided by a neural surrogate has been applied
to one-dimensional spherical criticality benchmarks~\cite{rlGrid}. In adjacent
problems, neural surrogates inside genetic algorithms are by now routine, for
core loading patterns~\cite{coreLoading} and for experiment
design~\cite{fnsSurrogate}. In the wider optimization literature the pattern is
mature and carries a name---surrogate-assisted evolutionary algorithms---with an
extensive body of work on how best to spend the expensive
evaluations~\cite{saeaSurvey}.

The contribution of this work is therefore not the pipeline. It is threefold.
First, we carry surrogate-accelerated group-structure optimization to full-core
fidelity with a fifteen-dimensional objective spanning reactivity, kinetics
parameters, flux and power, evaluated simultaneously for three core
states---an application well beyond the one-dimensional, single-quantity
benchmarks on which the idea has so far been demonstrated. Second, and more
importantly, we map where the metamodel can and cannot be trusted, excluding
three candidate explanations experimentally rather than arguing them away, and we
show that the residual limitation is a property of the target function rather
than of the model. Third, we quantify the behaviour that makes the verification
stage indispensable: a search driven by a surrogate is drawn preferentially
towards the regions where that surrogate is optimistic, so that increasing the
number of searches makes verification more necessary, not less.

The work builds directly on the multi-configuration genetic optimization of
Ref.~\cite{prevMulti}, from which the chromosome representation, the fitness
functions and the three core configurations are taken unchanged, and which in
turn concludes a line of work on this system~\cite{lfrGA2022,lfrGA2024,prevGA}
rooted in the multigroup structure search of Refs.~\cite{massoneGA,simmerGA}. The workflow adopted here and the one it
replaces are compared in Fig.~\ref{fig:flow}.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

Serpent~2 is used to generate, for each core configuration, both the reference
values of the integral parameters and the spatially homogenised fine-group
constants, evaluated at isothermal conditions with fuel and coolant at
$673$~K. The data are collapsed onto a fine-group structure of 30 groups whose
boundaries are extracted from the ECCO-33 grid, chosen so that few groups
describe the thermal and the fast regions where the Monte Carlo statistics are
poorest. That 30-group structure is the pool of boundaries from which a
candidate solution is built: a candidate is any subset of the 29 internal
boundaries, so that the design space contains $2^{29}\approx5.4\times10^{8}$
structures. A structure with $G$ groups is encoded, for the metamodel, as a
29-bit vector together with $G$ itself.

Three control-rod configurations are considered simultaneously: fully
withdrawn, partially inserted---the absorbing region facing the fuel over a
length of $28$~cm---and fully inserted. Quality is measured, for a candidate
structure $I$ and against the Monte Carlo reference, by five fitness functions
per configuration~\cite{prevMulti}:
%
\begin{equation}
  f_k^I = \left|k^I - k^\mathrm{MC}\right|\times10^5, \qquad
  f_{\beta}^I = \left|\beta_\mathrm{eff}^I - \beta_\mathrm{eff}^\mathrm{MC}\right|
  \times10^5, \qquad
  f_{\Lambda}^I = \frac{\Lambda_\mathrm{eff}^I -
  \Lambda_\mathrm{eff}^\mathrm{MC}}{\Lambda_\mathrm{eff}^\mathrm{MC}}\times10^2,
  \label{eqn:integral}
\end{equation}
%
for the multiplication factor, the effective delayed neutron fraction and the
effective neutron generation time, and
%
\begin{equation}
  f_P^I = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{J}\left(\frac{P_j^I-P_j^\mathrm{MC}}
  {P_j^\mathrm{MC}}\right)^{2}}, \qquad
  f_{\Phi}^I = \sqrt{\sum_{g=1}^{G}\sum_{j=1}^{J} w_{g,j}
  \left(\frac{\Phi_{g,j}^I-\Phi_{g,j}^\mathrm{MC}}
  {\Phi_{g,j}^\mathrm{MC}}\right)^{2}},
  \label{eqn:distributed}
\end{equation}
%
for the power and the flux distributions over the $J$ core assemblies, the flux
being weighted by the total importance $\phi\phi^{\ddagger}/v$. All five are
deviations from the reference, so smaller values are better.

Because the five quantities differ by orders of magnitude and in range of
variability, each is mapped onto $[1,100]$ by the linear scaling
%
\begin{equation}
  h_L : f \longrightarrow 1 + 99\,
  \frac{f-\min_{\forall I\in E}\left(f^I\right)}
       {\max_{\forall I\in E}\left(f^I\right)-\min_{\forall I\in E}\left(f^I\right)},
  \label{eqn:scaling}
\end{equation}
%
where $E$ is the set of solutions evaluated so far, and the joint fitness is the
product of the fifteen scaled terms, five for each of the three
configurations~\cite{prevMulti}. Additional controls are applied to the raw
values before scaling, clipping the reactivity and kinetics quantities from
below and taking a base-ten logarithm floored at unity for the flux.

\section{The Metamodel}
\label{sec:metamodel}

\subsection{Data}

The training set comprises 91\,723 distinct structures for which the neutronics
module of FRENETIC has been run over the three configurations, accumulated over
an initial space-filling pool and successive batches in which the configurations
to be simulated next were chosen by the disagreement of an ensemble of five
networks, averaged over the nine most difficult outputs. A further 1\,700 structures form an external validation
set that is never used in training, nor for early stopping, nor for any
hyperparameter decision; every accuracy figure quoted in this paper refers to
it.

\subsection{Architecture}

The fifteen outputs are not independent: they group naturally into five physical
families, each evaluated for the same three core states. The network mirrors
that structure. A shared trunk of three fully connected layers ($1024$, $512$,
$256$) receives the 29-bit encoding together with $G$; it feeds five
family-level necks ($128$, $64$), each of which feeds three configuration-level
branches ($32$) terminating in the corresponding output. Parameters are thus
shared to the extent that the physics shares them, and specialised where it does
not. An ablation over the same data gives a mean coefficient of determination of
$0.9852$ for a single-headed network, $0.9882$ when only the five families are
separated, and $0.9894$ for the full hierarchy.

Twelve of the fifteen outputs span several orders of magnitude and are learned
as $\log_{10}(y+10^{-6})$; the three power fitness functions are learned
directly. Predictions are averaged over an ensemble of five networks trained
from different initialisations~\cite{deepEnsembles}; the disagreement within
the ensemble also supplies the uncertainty used by the active-learning
acquisition.

\subsection{Training}

Networks are trained in PyTorch~\cite{pytorch} with AdamW~\cite{adamw} and no
weight decay, in four stages of decreasing learning rate ($3\times10^{-3}$,
$3\times10^{-4}$, $10^{-4}$ and $3\times10^{-5}$), each stopped on the
validation loss. The schedule matters considerably more than one might
expect: the same architecture trained at a constant $3\times10^{-3}$ with early
stopping reaches a median relative error of $0.715\%$, against $0.422\%$ with
the staged decay, both measured on the same data---a reduction of $41\%$, larger than the combined effect of
every architectural change described above. A control run with the decay but no
additional data reproduces the improvement exactly, confirming that it comes
from the schedule and not from the data.

\subsection{Accuracy}

Table~\ref{tab:accuracy} reports the accuracy per output and
Fig.~\ref{fig:scatter} the corresponding predicted-against-reference scatter.
The ensemble reaches a mean coefficient of determination of $0.99634$ and a
median relative error of $0.403\%$. Accuracy is not uniform: the power fitness
functions are predicted to better than $0.2\%$, whereas the flux ones are the
least accurate, at about $2.4\%$.

\subsection{How Much Data Is Enough}

Figure~\ref{fig:learning} shows the external error as a function of the training
set size, reconstructed by retraining on cumulative subsets of the
active-learning rounds in chronological order. The error falls steeply up to
about $53\,000$ configurations and then flattens: the last $36\,400$
configurations, forty per cent of the data set, buy a $7\%$ reduction in median
error, with fluctuations in both directions consistent with the training noise
of the reduced protocol used for this curve. Further active-learning rounds were
therefore not pursued.

\subsection{Where the Metamodel Fails, and Why}
\label{sec:limit}

The residual error is not spread evenly over the design space. It concentrates
at small values of the reactivity and kinetics fitness functions and, for the
flux, at large values. Since these are the structures that perform best, and
therefore exactly the ones an optimizer will visit, the behaviour deserves an
explanation rather than a caveat.

Three candidate explanations were tested and each was excluded by measurement;
Table~\ref{tab:hypotheses} summarises them. The labels carry no noise: of the
configurations present in more than one data file, 21\,440 in total, the spread
is $0.0000\%$ on every output, and an independent re-run of the solver
reproduces stored reference values to all digits. The regime is not
data-starved; on the contrary it contains two and a half times more training
points than the regime in which the model performs best. Finally, augmenting the 30 inputs with 65 physics-informed
descriptors---lethargy widths of the condensed groups, the variation of the
reference flux shape within each of them, and the widths of the groups
containing the sharpest spectral features---makes the model \emph{worse}
($0.473\%$ against $0.422\%$ on the same data), because a network of this
capacity can construct
such features internally where they help.

What remains is a property of the target itself. Searching the data set for
pairs of structures differing by exactly one boundary yields 54\,575 such pairs.
The median change in the base-ten logarithm of the fitness function across a
pair is $0.414$ decades in the lowest band of multiplication-factor deviations,
against $0.024$ decades in the highest---a factor of seventeen---and $0.395$
against $0.044$ for the generation time. Figure~\ref{fig:smallvalue} shows that
the model error follows this steepness across all five families, including the
flux, which is steepest at large values and is indeed least accurate there.

The physical reading is direct. A small fitness function means that the
condensed calculation reproduces the reference almost exactly, which it achieves
through a fine cancellation of errors across groups; cancellations are fragile,
and displacing one boundary destroys them. A smooth interpolator cannot follow a
function that jumps by half a decade under a one-bit change of its input. This
is not a deficiency that more data or a different architecture will remove, and
it is the reason why the reference solver retains the last word.

\section{The Optimization Workflow}
\label{sec:workflow}

\subsection{A Fitness That Does Not Move}
\label{sec:canonical}

The scaling of Eq.~(\ref{eqn:scaling}) uses the minimum and the maximum
encountered so far in the run. That choice keeps comparisons internally
consistent within a generation, but it has a consequence that matters as soon as
several independent searches are to be compared: the scale itself depends on how
much a run has explored. We measured this directly---a search of 500 generations
reports a worse final fitness than one of 300 on the same problem, purely
because it widened its own window.

We therefore define a \emph{canonical} fitness, in which the two extremes of
Eq.~(\ref{eqn:scaling}) are computed once from all 89\,723 configurations
already available and then held fixed. The canonical fitness is comparable
across searches, which multi-start requires, and it is also stationary within a
search. The latter has two further consequences: the genetic algorithm now
optimizes exactly the quantity by which its solutions are subsequently ranked,
which was previously not the case; and the re-evaluation of the whole population
at every generation, needed in the reference workflow to compensate for the
moving scale, becomes unnecessary.

\subsection{Multi-Start Search}

Because a single search samples a multimodal landscape only once, we run many
independent searches---``tribes''---differing only in their random seed. Each
uses a population of 100 over 100 generations, with two-point crossover, integer
random mutation at $0.05$ per gene, and a repair operator enforcing a number of
groups between 4 and 20. The search reproduces the scheme of
Ref.~\cite{prevMulti}---the chromosome representation, the repair operator and
the fitness functions---but not its implementation: we build it on the pymoo
library~\cite{pymoo} rather than on the coupled code of
Refs.~\cite{prevMulti,massoneGA}, and we use the canonical fitness in place of
the dynamically scaled one. Population and generation counts were fixed by
measurement: populations of 40 give a final fitness about twice as poor,
populations of 200 give no improvement, and the median tribe reaches within
$1\%$ of its own final value by generation 42, with only four tribes in a
hundred still improving after generation 90.

One implementation detail has consequences for reproducibility. The sampling and
mutation operators of this problem draw from the global random generator, which
the optimization library does not seed; the seed passed to the optimizer
therefore had no effect and successive runs were not reproducible. Seeding the
global generator explicitly before each tribe restores bit-level
reproducibility.

Tribes are independent and are executed in parallel. One hundred tribes take
$79$~s on eight processes---$0.79$~s per tribe, against $9.8$~s for the serial,
dynamically scaled formulation, a speed-up of a factor of twelve.

\subsection{The Reference Solver as Final Judge}

The best distinct candidates by canonical fitness are then evaluated with
FRENETIC, and the ranking reported is the one obtained from those true values.
No result presented in this paper is a metamodel prediction: the metamodel
decides what deserves a full-core calculation, and the full-core calculation
decides what is best.


\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Behaviour of the Search}

One hundred tribes produced 67 distinct structures. Figure~\ref{fig:search}
shows the convergence of all of them on the common canonical scale, the
distribution of the generation at which each tribe reaches within $1\%$ of its
own final value, and where the tribes land in the plane of group count against
fitness.

A quarter of the tribes finish within $5\%$ of the best predicted fitness, and
the median one is $15\%$ worse. Multi-start is therefore useful but not for the
reason one might expect: the plateau is reached often enough, and what a single
search cannot guarantee is reaching the \emph{right} plateau, as the following
section shows.

\subsection{Two Families of Solutions}

The 67 distinct structures do not form a single cloud. Clustering them by
Hamming distance separates two groups whose mean internal distance is $5.0$
boundaries against $10.4$ between them, and the separation is almost binary in
terms of specific boundaries: one family places fast-region cuts at $3.679$ and
$0.302$~MeV and includes a thermal cut at $5.4\times10^{-7}$~MeV, while the
other places them at $2.231$ and $0.111$~MeV instead
(Fig.~\ref{fig:structure}). Roughly half of the tribes converge to each: 53
against 47 in the run reported here.

The distinction is not one of group count---the two families span overlapping
ranges of $G$---and it is not an artefact of the metamodel. Verifying the six
best members of the poorer family with FRENETIC gives a median true fitness of
$1.055\times10^{7}$ against $9.105\times10^{6}$ for the better one, a genuine
gap of $16\%$; the metamodel predicts those six structures with a disagreement
of between $0.5\%$ and $2.4\%$, better than its agreement on the family it
prefers. The design space genuinely contains two competing basins, one of them
systematically inferior, and a single search has an even chance of ending in it
without any indication that it has done so.

\subsection{Verification and the Optimism of a Surrogate-Driven Search}

Table~\ref{tab:ranking} reports the fifteen verified candidates. The median
disagreement between predicted and true joint fitness is $3.0\%$, and the
metamodel's own first choice is also the best according to FRENETIC. That
agreement should not be over-read: the second-ranked structure is only $0.55\%$
behind the first in true fitness, far below the resolution of the metamodel, so
the correct identification of the leader is to a large extent fortunate.

More instructive is the direction of the error: thirteen of the fifteen
predictions are \emph{under}-estimates (Fig.~\ref{fig:bias}). This is not
random. A search minimising a predicted quantity is attracted both to genuinely
good structures and to structures the metamodel underrates, and cannot
distinguish between them. Increasing the number of tribes therefore increases
the number of opportunities to exploit the metamodel's optimism, which makes the
verification stage more necessary rather than less. The same figure shows where the error sits: eleven of the fifteen quantities
are predicted to better than $1.3\%$ on the proposed structures, while the
multiplication factor in the partially inserted configuration and the flux with
the rods half inserted reach median errors of $8.0\%$ and $6.9\%$. The first of
these is the small-value regime characterised above, appearing here in operation
rather than in diagnosis.

\subsection{The Optimized Structure}

The best verified structure has $G=18$ and a canonical fitness of
$8.5582\times10^{6}$, with internal boundaries at $3.679$, $0.302$, $0.1832$,
$0.1111$, $6.738\times10^{-2}$, $2.479\times10^{-2}$, $1.503\times10^{-2}$,
$9.119\times10^{-3}$, $5.531\times10^{-3}$, $3.355\times10^{-3}$,
$2.035\times10^{-3}$, $1.234\times10^{-3}$, $7.485\times10^{-4}$,
$4.540\times10^{-4}$, $3.043\times10^{-4}$, $9.166\times10^{-5}$ and
$5.4\times10^{-7}$~MeV. Like the solutions reported in
Ref.~\cite{prevMulti}, it uses more than fifteen groups, confirming that the
optimization is driven towards the upper end of the admissible range.

It is better than every one of the 124\,693 configurations computed to date, by
$15.8\%$ with respect to the best among them. Against structures built by the
obvious rules it is better by orders of magnitude: a structure with uniformly
spaced boundary indices and the same 19 boundaries is worse by a factor of
$1.2\times10^{4}$, and one with uniform lethargy spacing by a factor of
$5.1\times10^{2}$. Table~\ref{tab:standard} resolves those figures quantity by
quantity, which is necessary because a product of fifteen terms amplifies
moderate individual differences: the optimized structure wins decisively on the
delayed neutron fraction and on the multiplication factor in the partially
inserted configuration, loses on the delayed neutron fraction with the rods
fully inserted, and six of the fifteen terms saturate at their floor for all
three structures and thus carry no information in this regime.

We note in passing that a uniform-lethargy structure cannot be built with 19
groups on this master mesh: the 30 fine-group boundaries are not themselves
equally spaced in lethargy, so that equally spaced targets collapse onto
repeated boundaries and only 13 survive. The fine-group structure constrains
which ``natural'' coarse structures are constructible at all.

\subsection{How Many Groups Are Needed}

Repeating the search with the group count constrained gives the attainable
fitness as a function of $G$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff}), each point verified with
FRENETIC. The curve falls by a factor of $4.3$ between $G=8$ and $G=14$ and is
flat thereafter, the whole range from $G=14$ to $G=20$ lying within $7\%$.
Fourteen groups are therefore sufficient for this core and this set of fitness
functions, and the additional groups used by the best structure buy little. This
is the kind of question a metamodel answers and a direct search does not:
thirteen constrained optimizations cost four minutes on the surrogate and would
have cost some five thousand core-hours on the solver.

\subsection{Cost}

Table~\ref{tab:cost} gives the budget. Building the metamodel cost about
$3\,516$ core-hours of FRENETIC, whereas one tribe evaluated directly on the
solver would cost $383$. The metamodel therefore does not pay for itself below
about nine tribes---a point worth stating plainly, since for a single
optimization a direct search is cheaper. Above that threshold the advantage
compounds: the search reported here would have cost some $38\,000$ core-hours
and cost $0.6$, and the data set, once built, also supplied the constrained
searches described above at no additional cost.


\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Three limitations should be stated.

The verification stage is greedy. Candidates are sent to the solver in order of
predicted fitness, so the procedure only ever examines structures of which the
metamodel approves and cannot, by construction, detect a systematic bias in the
metamodel's landscape. We tested for such a bias in the one case where it was
plausible---the poorer of the two solution families---and found none, but that
test was deliberate and is not a property of the workflow. A diversity-aware
allocation of the verification budget, as is standard practice in the
surrogate-assisted optimization literature~\cite{saeaSurvey}, would remove the
blind spot.

The objective is less informative than it appears. Of the fifteen fitness
functions, those of the generation time sit at their floor for $90\%$ of the
configurations in the data set and the flux ones saturate among the good
structures, so that discrimination in the region where the optimization operates
comes from the multiplication factor, the power and two of the three delayed
neutron fraction terms. An immediate practical consequence is that the flux,
which is the quantity the metamodel predicts least accurately, has almost no
influence on the outcome, whereas the multiplication factor with the rods fully
inserted, which has the lowest coefficient of determination of all fifteen,
dominates the product. Effort spent on metamodel accuracy is best directed
there.

Finally, the metamodel is specific to this core, this fine-group structure and
these three control-rod configurations. The data set that supports it is its
principal cost, and transferring the approach to another system means paying
that cost again.


\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

A neural metamodel trained on 91\,723 full-core evaluations predicts fifteen
fitness functions for a candidate energy structure with a median relative error
of $0.403\%$, fast enough that one hundred independent genetic searches complete
in $79$~s. Verified by the reference solver, the resulting 18-group structure
improves on the best of 124\,693 previously computed configurations by $15.8\%$
and on standard constructions by orders of magnitude, and the attainable
accuracy is shown to saturate at about fourteen groups.

The more transferable results are the negative and the structural ones. The
metamodel's residual error is confined to a regime in which the target itself
changes by half a decade under a one-bit change of its input, and three
alternative explanations for it were excluded by measurement, including the
addition of physics-informed input features, which made the model worse. A
surrogate-driven search is systematically attracted to the surrogate's own
optimism, so that more searching strengthens rather than weakens the case for
verification. And the design space contains two genuinely separated families of
structures, one $16\%$ worse than the other, with an even chance of converging
to the wrong one---which is what multi-start buys, and what a single search,
however well converged, cannot reveal.
